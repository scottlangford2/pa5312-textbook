# Introduction to Program Evaluation

## Epigraphs

> "Not everything that can be counted counts, and not everything that counts can be counted."
> — William Bruce Cameron, *Informal Sociology* (1963)

> "Evaluation is the systematic application of social research procedures for assessing the conceptualization, design, implementation, and utility of social intervention programs."
> — Peter H. Rossi, Mark W. Lipsey, and Gary T. Henry, *Evaluation: A Systematic Approach* (paraphrasing their working definition)

## Opening Case: The Workforce Voucher in Hays County

Imagine that the commissioners of a fast-growing Central Texas county — call it Hays County, just south of Austin — adopted a workforce voucher three years ago. The program gives unemployed residents up to \$2,000 toward a short-term credential at a community college or trade school. The first year cost roughly \$1.4 million; the county funded it from a one-time budget surplus. That surplus is gone. The commissioners must now decide whether to fund the program permanently out of recurring revenue, shrink it, or end it.

The program director arrives at the budget hearing with a binder. It shows that 612 residents enrolled, 488 finished a credential, and "many" found jobs. A commissioner asks the obvious question: *would those people have found jobs anyway?* The director does not have an answer. Another commissioner notes that the local economy has been booming, and unemployment fell countywide over the same period — so maybe the program rode a wave rather than created one. A third commissioner simply wants to know whether the \$1.4 million bought more job placements than the same money spent on, say, expanding public transit to job centers.

Each commissioner is asking a different *evaluation question*, and none of them can be answered by the binder. This is the situation public managers find themselves in constantly: a program exists, money is at stake, decisions loom, and the available evidence describes activity rather than effect.

**Guiding Questions**

- What is the difference between *what a program did* and *what difference the program made*?
- Which type of evaluation answers each commissioner's question?
- How is evaluation different from the monitoring reports a manager already produces?

## Why This Chapter Matters

Public and nonprofit managers are accountable for results, not just effort. Legislatures, councils, boards, and funders increasingly ask programs to show evidence of effectiveness, and "we served a lot of people" is no longer a sufficient answer. Program evaluation is the craft of producing credible answers to questions about whether a program is needed, whether it is being run as intended, whether it produces the outcomes it promises, and whether those outcomes are worth the cost. This chapter maps the territory so that every later chapter — measurement, regression, difference-in-differences, cost-benefit analysis — has a place to attach.

> **Briefing:** Evaluation converts a program's *activity* into defensible claims about its *effect and value*, which is exactly what budget and policy decisions require.

## What Program Evaluation Is

Evaluation is the systematic, evidence-based assessment of a program's design, operation, results, and worth. Three words in that sentence carry weight. *Systematic* means we use explicit methods that others could inspect and reproduce, not impressions. *Evidence-based* means conclusions follow from data rather than from the enthusiasm of the program's champions. *Worth* means we are ultimately making a value judgment — useful, effective, cost-justified — and not merely describing.

A useful contrast is between an *output* and an *outcome*. An output is something the program produces and controls directly: 488 credentials issued. An outcome is a change in the world the program is trying to cause: residents employed, earning more, off public assistance. The binder in our case reports outputs and gestures vaguely at outcomes. Evaluation insists on the distinction.

> **Briefing:** Outputs are what the program *does*; outcomes are the *change* it is trying to produce. Confusing the two is the most common error in public reporting.

## Why Public Managers Need It: Evidence-Based Policy and Management

The movement often called *evidence-based policy* holds that decisions about creating, scaling, reforming, or ending programs should rest on the best available evidence about what works. For a manager, the parallel idea is *evidence-based management*: using systematic evidence — including evaluation findings — to run the program, not only to defend it after the fact.

This is not a demand for certainty. Evidence is always partial and contestable. But there is a large difference between a commissioner deciding on a hunch and a commissioner deciding from a study that honestly states what it found and what it could not establish. Evaluation does not remove judgment from public decisions; it disciplines that judgment with data.

## The Major Types of Evaluation

A program can be evaluated at different stages and from different angles. Rossi, Lipsey, and Henry (2019) organize these into a recognizable hierarchy of questions. The order is roughly the order in which the questions logically arise over a program's life.

### Needs Assessment

A *needs assessment* asks: is there a problem of sufficient size and nature to justify a program, and whom does it affect? Before the voucher existed, a needs assessment would have asked how many county residents were unemployed, what barriers kept them from work, and whether short-term credentials addressed those barriers. Skipping this step risks building a well-run program that solves a problem nobody had.

### Process (Implementation) Evaluation

A *process evaluation* asks: is the program operating as designed and reaching the intended people? It examines enrollment, dosage, fidelity, and reach. If only residents who were already job-ready enrolled, the program may "work" on paper while missing the people who needed it. Process evaluation is also the diagnostic that explains a disappointing outcome study: a program can fail because the *theory* was wrong or because the *implementation* was wrong, and only a process evaluation can tell them apart.

### Outcome Evaluation

An *outcome evaluation* measures whether the intended outcomes occurred among participants — employment rates, earnings — after the program. Crucially, outcome evaluation describes *what happened* to participants but does not by itself establish that the program *caused* it. The booming local economy could explain the employment gains entirely.

### Impact Evaluation

An *impact evaluation* asks the causal question directly: how much of the observed outcome was *caused by* the program, over and above what would have happened anyway? This requires a comparison to a credible counterfactual — what participants' outcomes would have been without the program. Chapter 3 develops this idea formally; for now, note that it is the question every commissioner in our case is circling.

### Cost Evaluation

Finally, *cost evaluation* relates effects to dollars. *Cost-effectiveness analysis* expresses results as cost per unit of outcome (cost per job placement). *Cost-benefit analysis* converts both costs and benefits to dollars and compares them, often as a benefit-cost ratio or net present value. Chapter 11 treats these in depth.

> **Briefing:** The five questions — *Is it needed? Is it run right? Did outcomes occur? Did the program cause them? Was it worth the cost?* — are distinct, and an answer to one is not an answer to another.

## Formative versus Summative

A cross-cutting distinction concerns *purpose*. *Formative* evaluation is conducted to improve a program while it is running; its audience is the program's managers and staff, and its spirit is "how do we make this better?" Needs and process evaluations are typically formative. *Summative* evaluation is conducted to judge a program's overall value for an external decision — continue, expand, cut, end. Impact and cost evaluations are typically summative. The same data can sometimes serve both, but the framing differs: the formative question is *how*, the summative question is *whether*.

## The Evaluator's Role and Stakeholders

Evaluation is never purely technical, because every program has *stakeholders* — people with a stake in the findings. In our case these include the commissioners, the program staff, the participants, the community colleges that receive the vouchers, and the taxpayers. Each may want different questions answered and may have a stake in a particular answer. A central professional obligation of the evaluator is to remain credible across these interests: to specify questions and methods *in advance*, to report what the data show even when it disappoints the program's champions, and to state limitations plainly. The evaluator serves the decision, not the program.

## How Evaluation Differs from Monitoring and from Research

Managers already produce *performance monitoring*: routine, ongoing tracking of indicators (enrollments this month, dollars spent). Monitoring is continuous, descriptive, and internally focused; it tells you whether the dashboard is green. Evaluation is episodic, explanatory, and judgment-oriented; it asks *why* the dashboard looks the way it does and *whether* the program deserves to continue. Monitoring tells you the voucher enrolled 612 people; evaluation tells you whether enrolling them changed their employment.

Evaluation also differs from *basic research*. Research seeks generalizable knowledge and is driven by theory and the investigator's curiosity. Evaluation is driven by a specific program and a specific decision; its first loyalty is *use*. The methods overlap heavily — and we will borrow research's tools throughout — but the purpose differs.

> **Briefing:** Monitoring asks "are we on track?"; research asks "what is generally true?"; evaluation asks "is *this* program worth it, and why?"

### Worked Example: Outputs versus Outcomes in Excel

Suppose the program office gives you a small spreadsheet: for each of four cohorts, the number who enrolled, the number who earned a credential, and the number employed six months later. We want a quick, honest summary that separates outputs from an outcome *rate* — while remembering that a rate is still not an impact estimate.

Enter the data in columns A–D (rows 1–5 as a header plus four cohorts). In Excel:

- In **E2**, completion rate: `=C2/B2`, formatted as a percentage via **Home → Number → Percent Style**.
- In **F2**, employment rate among completers: `=D2/C2`.
- Fill **E2:F2** down to row 5 by selecting the cells and double-clicking the fill handle.
- In **B6**, a column total with `=SUM(B2:B5)`; copy across to **D6**.
- In **E7**, a program-wide completion rate computed from totals, `=C6/B6` — note this is *not* the same as averaging the four cohort rates, a distinction we revisit in Chapter 5.

| Cohort | Enrolled (B) | Completed (C) | Employed @6mo (D) | Completion % (E) | Employed % of completers (F) |
|--------|-------------:|--------------:|------------------:|-----------------:|------------------------------:|
| Spring | 160 | 132 | 95 | 82.5% | 72.0% |
| Summer | 158 | 121 | 80 | 76.6% | 66.1% |
| Fall   | 150 | 124 | 91 | 82.7% | 73.4% |
| Winter | 144 | 111 | 70 | 77.1% | 63.1% |
| **Total** | **612** | **488** | **336** | **79.7%** | **68.9%** |

The spreadsheet now cleanly reports outputs (enrolled, completed) and an outcome *rate* (employed). What it cannot tell us is the counterfactual employment rate — what these residents' employment would have been with no voucher. That gap is the entire subject of impact evaluation.

> **Returning to the Case:** The director's binder answered a *process* and *outcome* question — the program ran and 336 completers were employed. The commissioners are asking *impact* and *cost* questions. The disagreement at the hearing is not about the numbers; it is about which evaluation question the numbers can answer.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Reporting outputs as if they were outcomes.** "488 credentials" is an output; it tells you nothing about employment caused by the program.
- **Ignoring the counterfactual.** A before-after improvement during an economic boom may reflect the boom, not the program. Without a comparison, an outcome is not an impact.
- **Choosing the type of evaluation to flatter the program.** A struggling program's champions often prefer a process evaluation ("we worked hard"); skeptics demand impact. The decision at hand should dictate the type, not the politics.
- **Treating monitoring dashboards as evaluation.** Green indicators show activity, not effect.
- **Letting stakeholders set the conclusion in advance.** Specify questions and methods before seeing results.

## Practice and Application

1. **Classify the questions.** For the Hays County voucher, write each commissioner's question and label it as needs, process, outcome, impact, or cost. Explain why.
2. **Outputs vs. outcomes (Excel).** Recreate the worked-example spreadsheet. Add a column **G** computing each cohort's employed share of *all enrollees* (`=D2/B2`). Explain in two sentences why E, F, and G can tell three different stories about the "same" program.
3. **Formative or summative?** A nonprofit director wants to know which of two intake procedures fills classes faster; a city council wants to know whether to keep funding the nonprofit. Classify each request and identify its audience.
4. **County panel (Excel).** Open the Texas County Panel. Pick a single county and chart its unemployment rate across the 2000–2024 presidential years using **Insert → Line Chart**. In a short paragraph, explain why this trend illustrates the counterfactual problem: if a county program launched in 2016, why can't this chart alone show the program's impact?
5. **Distinguish the disciplines.** In one paragraph each, describe how performance monitoring, basic research, and program evaluation would each approach the city sales-tax data in the City Finance Panel differently.

## Transition to Chapter 2

The commissioners' confusion stems partly from a missing artifact: nobody ever wrote down *how* the voucher was supposed to turn dollars into jobs. That chain of reasoning — inputs to activities to outputs to outcomes to impacts, resting on stated assumptions — is called a program's *theory of change*, and its visual form is a *logic model*. Chapter 2 shows how to build one, how to read the evaluation questions and indicators directly off of it, and how to spot the broken links that doom programs before the first dollar is spent.
